## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The City of Austin's Transportation & Public Works Department (TPW) is the largest operational department in the city, managing over 2,000 lane miles of roadway, 100+ bridges, a multi-billion-dollar Capital Improvement Program (CIP), and the city's stormwater and drainage infrastructure. Formed in 2020 through the merger of Austin Transportation and Austin Public Works, TPW administers the 2020 Mobility Bond ($460M) alongside legacy bond programs totaling more than $1.1B in active capital investment.

Austin faces a compounding infrastructure challenge: rapid population growth (now 1M+ residents), aging assets, chronic underfunding of maintenance relative to the capital backlog, and a geography that places low-income and minority communities disproportionately at the intersection of deferred maintenance, flood risk, and traffic violence. This briefing covers:

1. Department overview and organizational structure
2. Budget and financial performance
3. Infrastructure condition and capital projects
4. Vision Zero and transportation safety
5. Drainage and flood infrastructure
6. Equity, displacement, and service distribution
7. Key findings and policy implications

## SECTION 1: DEPARTMENT OVERVIEW

### Mission
TPW's mission is to build and maintain a safe, reliable, accessible, and sustainable transportation and infrastructure system that supports Austin's quality of life and economic vitality.

### Formation
TPW was created in October 2020 by consolidating:
- **Austin Transportation Department (ATD)** — traffic engineering, Vision Zero, active transportation, smart mobility
- **Austin Public Works (APW)** — roads, bridges, drainage, facilities, right-of-way management

The merger was intended to eliminate redundancy, streamline capital delivery, and better integrate safety and infrastructure functions.

### Organizational Structure
TPW is organized into the following major divisions:

| Division | Primary Function |
|---|---|
| Infrastructure Management | Roadway and bridge maintenance, inspections |
| Capital Delivery Services | CIP project management, contractor oversight |
| Vision Zero | Traffic safety program, High Injury Network |
| Active Transportation | Sidewalks, bike lanes, urban trails |
| Smart Mobility | Traffic signals, data, technology |
| Drainage & Waterways | Stormwater infrastructure, flood mitigation |
| Right-of-Way Management | Permits, utilities, encroachments |
| Facilities Management | City-owned buildings and grounds |

### Staffing
TPW employs approximately 1,100–1,300 FTEs, making it one of the largest departments in the city workforce. Vacancy rates in skilled trades and civil engineering have been a persistent operational challenge, with some years exceeding 15–20% vacancy in key roles.

### Revenue Sources
- **Property tax / General Fund** — funds day-to-day operations, maintenance, personnel
- **Drainage Utility Fee** — charged to property owners based on impervious cover; dedicated to stormwater/flood infrastructure
- **Voter-approved bonds** — capital construction and major rehabilitation projects
- **Federal grants** — FHWA, RAISE, Safe Streets, BUILD grants; significant infusion from IIJA (Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, 2021)
- **State funds** — TxDOT partnerships and pass-through grants

### Bond Program Status
Austin voters have approved multiple transportation and infrastructure bonds:

| Bond Program | Amount | Approval Year | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 Mobility Bond | $460M | 2020 | Active; projects underway |
| 2016 Mobility Bond | $720M | 2016 | Substantially complete |

| GO Bond (facilities) | Varies | Various | Ongoing |

The 2020 Mobility Bond funds: corridor improvements, safety/Vision Zero, urban trails, sidewalks, and local arterial improvements.

### Capital Backlog
Austin's infrastructure capital backlog — the gap between needed investment and funded investment — is estimated at **$3–5B+** for roads, bridges, and drainage alone. The backlog grows approximately $100–200M/year as assets age faster than the maintenance budget can address.

## SECTION 3: INFRASTRUCTURE CONDITION AND CAPITAL PROJECTS

### Roadway Network
- **~2,000+ lane miles** of city-maintained roads (excluding TxDOT-maintained state highways)
- **Pavement Condition Index (PCI)**: Austin targets a systemwide average PCI above 70 ("good"); as of recent reporting, the average PCI has been in the mid-to-upper 60s, indicating a system under maintenance stress
- **Arterials vs. residential**: Major arterials generally receive more consistent maintenance; residential streets in older neighborhoods (particularly East Austin, South Austin, and far north/east corridors) show disproportionate deferred maintenance

### Bridges
- **100+ city-maintained bridges**
- Federal bridge inspection standards require biennial inspection; Austin has generally maintained compliance
- A subset of bridges carry "structurally deficient" or "functionally obsolete" ratings nationally — Austin's specific count fluctuates but typically includes a handful of structures on the watch list
- Major capital projects include rehabilitation of aging creek crossings

### Active Transportation Infrastructure
- **Sidewalk gap inventory**: Austin has an estimated **1,700–2,000+ miles of sidewalk gaps** citywide — a persistent equity issue as gaps are concentrated in lower-income neighborhoods and near schools
- **Urban trails**: Barton Creek Greenbelt, Walnut Creek, Shoal Creek, and other trail corridors managed by TPW/PARD in partnership
- **Bike network**: Growing but fragmented; Austin's protected bike lane network remains limited relative to peer cities

### Active Capital Projects (Selected)

## SECTION 4: VISION ZERO AND TRANSPORTATION SAFETY

### Program Background
Austin adopted **Vision Zero** in 2016, committing to eliminate all traffic fatalities and serious injuries. The original goal was zero deaths by 2025 — a target Austin did not achieve. Annual traffic fatalities in Austin have ranged from **~100–120 deaths/year** in recent years, with serious injuries in the hundreds annually.

### High Injury Network (HIN)
The High Injury Network identifies the ~8% of Austin streets where the majority of severe and fatal crashes occur. Key HIN characteristics:
- Concentrated on high-speed arterials and corridors: Rundberg Ln, E. William Cannon, US 183 (city-managed segments), N. Lamar, S. Congress, E. 7th/MLK
- **Disproportionately located in lower-income and minority neighborhoods** — particularly East and North Austin
- Pedestrian and cyclist fatalities account for a growing share of total deaths (often 25–35%)

### Contributing Factors
- **Speeding**: Texas state law historically limited Austin's ability to lower speed limits without TxDOT approval; recent legislative changes have created some flexibility
- **Road design**: Many HIN corridors feature wide travel lanes, limited pedestrian refuge, poor lighting, and high-speed access points designed for vehicle throughput over safety
- **Impaired and distracted driving**
- **Equity gaps in infrastructure**: Sidewalk gaps and poor lighting on HIN corridors mean pedestrians — often transit-dependent, lower-income residents — face elevated risk

### Recent Actions
- Deployment of **Automated Speed Safety Cameras (ASSC)** in school zones (authorized by state law)
- **Corridor safety retrofits**: Raised crosswalks, pedestrian refuges, signal timing changes
- **Community safety grants** targeting HIN neighborhoods
- **Safe Routes to School** infrastructure improvements

### Equity Dimension
Traffic fatalities in Austin fall disproportionately on:
- **Black and Hispanic residents** (overrepresented as pedestrian/cyclist victims relative to population share)
- **Lower-income neighborhoods** (where HIN intersects with affordable housing corridors — e.g., Rundberg/North Lamar, East MLK, E. William Cannon)
- **Renters and transit users** who walk and bike to access transit stops

## SECTION 5: DRAINAGE AND FLOOD INFRASTRUCTURE

### Geographic Context
Austin sits in **Flash Flood Alley**, one of the most flood-prone regions in the United States. The city's topography — limestone karst terrain, steep watersheds, and impervious urban surfaces — produces rapid, high-volume runoff during storm events.

### Key Watersheds and Flood Risk
| Watershed | Key Corridor | Risk Level | Notable Events |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion Creek | SE Austin | Very High | 2013 Halloween Flood (13 deaths) |
| Williamson Creek | South/SW Austin | High | Repeated flooding events |
| Shoal Creek | Central Austin | High | 1981 Memorial Day Flood; repeated events |
| Walnut Creek | Northeast Austin | Moderate–High | Increasing risk with development |
| Slaughter Creek | South Austin | Moderate | Growth-driven increases |

### Drainage Utility
The **Drainage Utility Enterprise Fund** is the primary funding mechanism for stormwater infrastructure:
- Funded by **impervious cover-based fees** on all Austin properties
- Budget: approximately $80–100M/year
- Administers: drainage system maintenance, capital projects, floodplain management, watershed protection

### Flood Mitigation Programs
- **Watershed Protection Department (WPD)** — technically a separate department but closely coordinated with TPW on capital projects and right-of-way work
- **Onion Creek Buy-Out Program** — ongoing voluntary property acquisition in the 100-year floodplain; one of the largest municipal buyout programs in Texas
- **Regional Stormwater Management** — cost-sharing with neighboring jurisdictions for shared watershed infrastructure
- **Green Stormwater Infrastructure** — bioswales, rain gardens, permeable pavement; growing but limited in scale relative to need

### Equity Dimension
Flood risk in Austin is not randomly distributed:
- **Lower-income neighborhoods** are disproportionately located in floodplains — a legacy of historical exclusionary zoning that pushed affordable housing to flood-prone areas
- **East Austin** (historically Black and Hispanic): multiple flood-prone corridors; residents face both flood risk and displacement pressure as the area gentrifies
- **Rundberg/Windsor Hills area (NE Austin)**: lower-income, high minority population, drainage infrastructure deficiencies
- **Manufactured housing communities** are disproportionately located in flood-prone areas and face the greatest displacement risk when buyout programs are implemented
- Infrastructure investment in flood mitigation can paradoxically **accelerate displacement** by reducing flood risk, increasing desirability, and driving up property values without corresponding affordability protections

## SECTION 6: EQUITY, DISPLACEMENT, AND SERVICE DISTRIBUTION

### The Infrastructure-Displacement Nexus
Public works investment is not equity-neutral. In a rapidly gentrifying city like Austin, infrastructure improvements can trigger a cycle:

> **Investment → Amenity uplift → Property value increase → Displacement of low-income residents**

This dynamic has been documented on corridors including:
- **East Cesar Chavez / E. 6th Street** — street improvements preceded rapid gentrification
- **Airport Blvd** — ongoing corridor investment coincides with rising rents in adjacent neighborhoods
- **North Lamar / Domain area** — active transportation improvements linked to premium real estate marketing

### Service Distribution Disparities
Analysis of TPW service distribution reveals persistent inequities:

| Service Category | High-Income Areas | Lower-Income Areas |
|---|---|---|
| Pavement condition (avg PCI) | Higher (75+) | Lower (55–65) |
| Sidewalk coverage | More complete | Significant gaps |
| Signalized intersections (maintained) | Higher density | Sparser network |
| Bike infrastructure | Growing investment | Limited, fragmented |
| Drainage infrastructure age | Newer (post-development) | Older, more deferred |
| Response time (maintenance requests) | Faster average | Slower average |

*Note: These are indicative patterns based on published studies and budget analyses; specific figures require verification against Austin 311 data and capital spending GIS analysis.*

### Demographic Intersections
- **East Austin** (ZIP codes 78702, 78721, 78723): historically Black and Hispanic; now rapidly gentrifying; infrastructure investment is accelerating displacement
- **North Lamar/Rundberg corridor** (78753): high Hispanic population, poverty concentration, HIN designation; infrastructure investment is critical for safety but must be paired with anti-displacement policy
- **South Austin** (78745, 78748): mixed-income; flood risk in lower-lying areas affects affordable housing stock

### ADCAP Connection: Infrastructure + Affordability
The nexus between TPW's work and affordable housing preservation is direct and underrecognized:

1. **Drainage buyouts without affordability protection**: Onion Creek buyouts displace low-income homeowners and renters; replacement housing options are limited
2. **Corridor investment triggers displacement**: Bond-funded street improvements increase land values; without community land trust or affordable housing preservation (per ADCAP framework), residents are displaced
3. **Sidewalk and safety gaps as displacement indicators**: Areas with chronic infrastructure underinvestment signal where affordability remains but also where displacement pressure is building
4. **Opportunity**: TPW capital projects could be paired with ADCAP-style affordability preservation tools — using infrastructure investment as a trigger for anti-displacement policy intervention

### Recommended Policy Linkages
- **Displacement Impact Review** for major TPW corridor projects (similar to environmental review)
- **Community Benefits Agreements** on large capital contracts requiring local hiring and small/minority business participation
- **Affordable housing siting** along corridors receiving mobility investment (transit-oriented development model)
- **Equitable Service Standards**: adopt explicit PCI, sidewalk coverage, and drainage maintenance targets disaggregated by income/race demographics

## SECTION 7: KEY FINDINGS AND POLICY IMPLICATIONS

### Findings Summary

**1. Infrastructure Backlog is Growing**
Austin's capital backlog exceeds $3–5B and grows annually. Maintenance appropriations have not kept pace with asset aging, shifting costs to future bond programs and future taxpayers.

**2. Safety Progress is Insufficient**
Vision Zero fatality targets were not met. The High Injury Network remains concentrated in lower-income corridors. Without structural redesign of high-speed arterials and speed reduction, incremental interventions will not achieve zero deaths.

**3. Flood Risk is Concentrated and Racialized**
Flood-prone areas in Austin are disproportionately lower-income and minority. Drainage investment is critical but must be paired with anti-displacement protections to avoid making flood mitigation a vector for gentrification.

**4. Service Inequities Persist**
Pavement quality, sidewalk coverage, and drainage infrastructure quality are lower in lower-income and minority neighborhoods — a legacy of historical disinvestment that active capital programs are only beginning to address.

**5. Infrastructure Investment Can Drive Displacement**
Austin lacks systematic policy to pair major public works investment with affordability preservation. This gap means that corridor improvements, safety investments, and flood mitigation — all ostensibly beneficial — can accelerate displacement without complementary affordable housing policy.

### Policy Recommendations

| Priority | Recommendation | Lead | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Adopt Equity-in-Infrastructure framework with disaggregated performance targets | TPW + Equity Office | 0–12 months |
| High | Require Displacement Impact Review for all capital corridor projects >$5M | TPW + Planning | 0–18 months |
| High | Accelerate sidewalk gap closure in equity priority zones | TPW | Ongoing |
| Medium | Pair Onion Creek buyout investments with ADCAP-style replacement housing fund | TPW + Housing | 12–24 months |
| Medium | Expand Vision Zero interventions on HIN corridors with structural redesign | TPW | 12–36 months |
| Medium | Establish Community Benefits Agreement template for major TPW contracts | TPW + OPO | 12–24 months |
| Long-term | Bond program development that explicitly targets equity gaps | TPW + City Council | 24–48 months |
