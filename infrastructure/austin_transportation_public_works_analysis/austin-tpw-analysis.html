Situation Overview & Key Policy Leverage Points

Page 2 of 6

The Situation

TPW was formed in October 2020 through the consolidation of the Austin Transportation Department and Austin Public Works, creating a unified department overseeing the full lifecycle of Austin's built transportation and drainage environment. With approximately 1,100–1,300 FTEs and a combined annual budget of $350–450M across all funds, it is the largest operational department in city government. Its asset portfolio spans over 2,000 lane miles of roadway, more than 100 bridges, a citywide drainage network, thousands of traffic signals, and an expanding urban trail and sidewalk system.

The department operates at the intersection of three compounding citywide pressures. First, rapid growth: Austin has surpassed one million residents, placing mounting demand on infrastructure that was built for a substantially smaller city. Second, historic underinvestment: maintenance appropriations have chronically lagged asset aging, producing a capital backlog that now exceeds $3–5B and grows annually. Third, structural inequity: the communities bearing the greatest burden of deferred maintenance, traffic violence, and flood risk are disproportionately low-income and minority — a pattern rooted in historical policy decisions and not random in its distribution.

Significant new resources are in the system. The 2020 Mobility Bond ($460M), legacy bond programs totaling $1.1B+ in active capital investment, and federal IIJA grants flowing through multiple competitive programs represent a generational influx of infrastructure capital. The challenge is not the absence of investment — it is the absence of policy frameworks that direct investment equitably and prevent it from accelerating displacement in the communities it enters.

Challenge 1 — Capital Backlog & Deferred Maintenance ($3–5B+)

Austin's infrastructure is aging faster than the maintenance budget can address. The capital backlog — the accumulated gap between the investment required to maintain infrastructure in a state of good repair and the funding actually appropriated — is estimated at $3–5B+ for roads, bridges, and drainage alone, growing approximately $100–200M per year. This is not a temporary shortfall; it is a structural feature of how Austin has historically financed infrastructure. Bond programs fund new construction and major rehabilitation, but routine maintenance is funded through the General Fund — and those appropriations have consistently fallen short of what the asset base requires.

The consequence is a compounding burden: each new bond cycle inherits a larger deferred backlog, raises public expectations with new infrastructure, and simultaneously leaves existing assets in declining condition. Austin targets a systemwide average Pavement Condition Index (PCI) of 70 or above; current estimates place the average in the mid-to-upper 60s. The gap is not uniform — arterials in higher-income areas receive more consistent maintenance, while residential streets in East Austin, North Austin, and older South Austin neighborhoods show the highest deterioration rates. Without a dedicated maintenance funding mechanism — separate from bond programs and insulated from annual budget competition — the backlog will continue to grow regardless of how many new bonds are approved.

What this looks like on the ground: Potholes that take months to repair in lower-income neighborhoods. Creek crossings that flood with each major storm because drainage culverts have not been upsized in decades. Bridges on a structural watch list because inspection findings have not triggered funded rehabilitation. These are not isolated failures — they are the predictable output of a system that builds faster than it maintains.

Challenge 2 — Vision Zero: Missed Targets, High-Risk Corridors

Austin adopted Vision Zero in 2016, committing to eliminate all traffic fatalities and serious injuries by 2025. That goal was not achieved. Annual traffic fatalities have ranged from approximately 100–120 deaths per year throughout the Vision Zero period, with no sustained downward trend. Pedestrian and cyclist fatalities account for 25–35% of the total — a share that is growing.

The High Injury Network (HIN) — the approximately 8% of Austin streets where the majority of severe and fatal crashes occur — is not randomly distributed across the city. It runs through the corridors that serve Austin's lowest-income, most transit-dependent residents: Rundberg Lane in North Austin, East MLK Jr. Boulevard and Airport Boulevard in East Austin, East William Cannon Drive in South Austin, and segments of North Lamar Boulevard. The people most likely to be killed or seriously injured while traveling in Austin are those who have the fewest transportation alternatives.

The primary drivers are structural: high-speed road geometry with wide travel lanes and minimal pedestrian accommodation; sidewalk gaps that force pedestrians into traffic lanes; poor lighting; and high-speed access points concentrated in corridors that carry heavy pedestrian volumes. Austin has deployed the low-cost interventions — signage, paint, automated speed cameras in school zones, safety grants. What it has not done consistently is fund structural redesign of HIN corridors: lane narrowing, raised pedestrian crossings, protected bike facilities, and pedestrian refuge islands. These are what peer Vision Zero cities identify as the primary driver of sustained fatality reduction. Without them, Austin will continue averaging 100+ deaths per year indefinitely.

Challenge 3 — Flood Infrastructure: Equity & Displacement

Austin sits in Flash Flood Alley, one of the most flood-prone regions in the United States. Limestone karst terrain, steep watersheds, and dense impervious surfaces combine to produce rapid, extreme runoff — conditions intensified by population growth and upstream development. The Onion Creek watershed in Southeast Austin, Williamson Creek in South Austin, Shoal Creek in Central Austin, and Walnut Creek in Northeast Austin all carry histories of repeated, severe flooding events.

The equity dimension is that Austin's flood risk is not randomly distributed. Flood-prone areas are disproportionately lower-income and minority — a direct legacy of exclusionary zoning and housing market discrimination that concentrated affordable housing in floodplains when higher-ground development was effectively restricted. The 2013 Halloween Flood on Onion Creek, which killed 13 people, was not an anomaly; it was the predictable result of manufactured housing sited in a 100-year floodplain over decades of underregulated development.

The Onion Creek Voluntary Buyout Program — financed through the Drainage Utility Enterprise Fund (~$80–100M/year) — has relocated hundreds of households from the floodplain since 2013. But flood-risk reduction has come at a significant social cost: no systematic replacement housing program exists. Displaced residents, predominantly lower-income homeowners and renters with limited capacity to navigate Austin's housing market, have in many cases left the city entirely. Drainage capital investment has also historically tracked new development — newer, wealthier areas received newer systems, while older East and North Austin corridors carry aging infrastructure that has not been systematically upsized.

The displacement paradox: Flood mitigation investment increases neighborhood desirability and drives up property values. Without affordability protections, the residents a buyout was designed to protect are subsequently displaced by the market conditions the investment helped create. Protecting communities from floods while pricing them out of the city is not a successful outcome — it is a transfer of harm, not an elimination of it.

Infrastructure as a Displacement Vector

Challenge 4 — Infrastructure as a Displacement Vector

Every major TPW capital project — corridor improvements, sidewalk buildout, drainage upgrades, trail extensions, safety retrofits — increases the attractiveness of adjacent neighborhoods and, with it, adjacent property values. In a rapidly gentrifying city with limited affordable housing stock and no systematic displacement protections, public infrastructure investment is not equity-neutral. It is a documented driver of displacement in the communities it ostensibly serves.

The pattern has been observed repeatedly in Austin. Street improvements along East Cesar Chavez preceded a wave of rapid gentrification. Airport Boulevard is actively receiving infrastructure investment while adjacent block-level rents rise. North Lamar improvements appear in luxury real estate marketing materials. The mechanism is well understood: bond-funded investment creates an amenity premium; the premium attracts private capital; private capital raises land values; and without anti-displacement intervention, lower-income renters and small businesses are priced out before they can benefit from the infrastructure their tax dollars funded.

Austin currently has no systematic mechanism to evaluate displacement risk before approving capital projects, and no policy requiring that infrastructure investment corridors be paired with affordability preservation tools. Each corridor improvement and safety retrofit is approved on its infrastructure merits alone. The displacement consequences — which are predictable, documentable, and in many cases preventable — are treated as an externality rather than a policy responsibility.

Closing Analysis

The performance dashboard presented on the preceding page reveals a department investing at scale but not yet investing with sufficient strategic equity orientation. The distinction between operational deficits and policy gaps is important. The operational deficits — fatalities, pavement deterioration, sidewalk gaps, a growing capital backlog — are resource problems. They require sustained, multi-year funding commitments to close and will not yield to short-term interventions alone.

The policy gaps are a different category of problem entirely. The absence of a Displacement Impact Review requirement, the lack of replacement housing plans paired with flood buyout programs, and the nonexistence of equity-disaggregated service standards are not funding shortfalls — they are institutional absences. They can be addressed through directive, framework adoption, and interagency coordination. They do not require new appropriations to initiate. This asymmetry matters: the corrective actions most immediately available are also among the most consequential, because the frameworks adopted now will govern how billions of dollars of existing bond and federal grant funding are deployed over the next decade.

The 2020 Mobility Bond is actively deploying capital into Austin's highest-displacement-risk corridors today. Federal IIJA funds are being drawn against projects already in design. Each cycle without an equity and displacement framework in place is another set of corridor projects committed without a displacement screen — and another set of communities improved on paper while being priced out in practice.

The core opportunity: TPW has the resources, institutional authority, and active capital program to begin closing these gaps now. The four structural challenges documented in this analysis are serious — but none are intractable. Each has a known policy intervention that peer cities and programs have demonstrated at scale. The question is not whether Austin can address them. It is whether the institutional will exists to do so before the current capital deployment window closes.
