## BUDGET AND FINANCE

**Q: TPW's budget seems large. Is Austin overspending on transportation?**
A: Austin's total TPW budget of ~$350–450M sounds substantial, but nearly all of it is committed — bond draws are voter-authorized, drainage fees are dedicated by charter, and personnel costs are fixed. The discretionary operating margin is thin. The more important story is the $3–5B capital backlog: Austin is systematically underspending on maintenance relative to the pace of asset aging.

**Q: Why does Austin keep going back to voters for bonds if we're already spending hundreds of millions?**
A: Bonds fund capital construction — building new things or major rehabilitation of existing assets. They cannot legally be used for routine operations or maintenance. Austin's maintenance appropriations have chronically lagged behind what's needed to extend the life of capital assets. So each bond cycle inherits more deferred maintenance from the last one.

**Q: How does Austin compare to peer cities on infrastructure spending per capita?**
A: Austin's infrastructure investment per capita is broadly comparable to peer Texas cities but below national high-performers. The more meaningful comparison is spending vs. need — Austin's growing population, flood geography, and aging inner-city infrastructure create above-average need that the current budget does not fully address. *(Verify with ASCE and FHWA benchmarks.)*

## VISION ZERO AND SAFETY

**Q: Why haven't Austin's traffic fatality numbers come down despite Vision Zero?**
A: Vision Zero goals require structural road redesign — narrowing lanes, eliminating slip ramps, reducing crossing distances, lowering speed limits. Austin has primarily pursued lower-cost interventions (paint, signs, education, signals). These have some effect but are insufficient to move fatality numbers materially. The High Injury Network requires physical redesign of the roads themselves, which is expensive and politically contested because it typically reduces vehicle throughput.

**Q: Aren't pedestrian fatalities partly due to pedestrian behavior?**
A: The research is clear: road design is the primary driver of pedestrian safety outcomes, not individual behavior. When roads are designed for 50+ mph vehicle speeds, pedestrian fatalities are predictable regardless of behavior. Safe road design — appropriate speeds, visible crossings, pedestrian refuge — protects people even when individuals make mistakes. That's the premise of Vision Zero.

**Q: Why are speed cameras only in school zones? Can't we expand them?**
A: Texas state law has historically restricted municipal use of automated speed enforcement. Legislative changes have provided some flexibility. Austin deployed cameras in school zones as the first authorized use case. Expansion requires additional state authorization and/or local political will to pursue legislative change. Some peer cities nationally have much broader automated enforcement programs.

**Q: Is Vision Zero working anywhere?**
A: Yes — Oslo, Helsinki, and several other European cities have achieved or approached zero traffic deaths using structural redesign approaches. In the U.S., Hoboken, NJ and some NYC corridors have shown progress. The common factor is sustained investment in physical road redesign paired with speed reduction, not just education campaigns.

## FLOOD AND DRAINAGE

**Q: Austin keeps flooding in the same places. Why can't we fix it?**
A: Some flood risk in Austin is irreducible given the geography — Flash Flood Alley produces extreme rainfall events that exceed any practical engineering capacity. However, upstream development is significantly worsening peak flood flows in many watersheds. The Onion Creek buyout program addresses the most dangerous residential exposures. For structural mitigation, detention basins and green infrastructure can reduce but not eliminate risk. Adequate funding and regional coordination (with Travis County, LCRA) are key constraints.

**Q: Why are poor people's neighborhoods still flooding when we have a drainage utility?**
A: The Drainage Utility Fund is real and significant, but capital allocation has historically followed development patterns — newer, wealthier areas with more recent development got more recently built drainage systems. Older neighborhoods in East and North Austin often have aging drainage infrastructure that predates modern standards. A drainage equity audit is needed to assess whether capital investment matches risk and need, rather than just following the path of least institutional resistance.

**Q: If we buy out flood properties, where do those residents go?**
A: That's the core problem. The Onion Creek buyout program has acquired hundreds of parcels, but there is no systematic replacement housing program. Evidence suggests many displaced households leave Austin entirely — to Round Rock, Pflugerville, or other suburbs — because they cannot afford to rent or buy in Austin after receiving buyout proceeds. This is a displacement outcome packaged as a safety intervention.

## EQUITY AND DISPLACEMENT

**Q: Isn't public investment in underserved neighborhoods a good thing? Why are you framing it as a displacement risk?**
A: Investment in underserved neighborhoods is necessary and overdue. The problem is investment without protection. When the city improves a corridor in East Austin without simultaneously ensuring that existing low-income residents can remain — through affordable housing preservation, community land trusts, anti-displacement zoning — the benefits of that investment accrue to future higher-income residents, not the people who lived there through the years of disinvestment.

**Q: Doesn't the market just work? If an area becomes more desirable, isn't that success?**
A: Rising desirability is only success if the people who lived there can share in it. In Austin's current market, without affordability protections, corridor improvement typically produces rapid rent increases and displacement within 3–7 years of major capital investment. The existing community is replaced rather than uplifted. That is not the stated goal of public investment in equity-priority areas.

**Q: What specifically would a Displacement Impact Review look like?**
A: A DIR would require, for any capital project over $5M in a designated equity-priority area, an analysis of: (1) projected property value impact; (2) number of low-income renter and owner households within the impact zone; (3) recommended mitigation measures (e.g., preservation of affordable units, right-to-return provisions, community land trust acquisition); and (4) referral to Housing Department for affordability response. It mirrors the environmental review process already required for federal projects.

**Q: How does this connect to ADCAP?**
A: ADCAP provides a framework for affordable housing preservation using public, private, and nonprofit capital. TPW's corridor investment creates the trigger condition that makes ADCAP intervention both necessary and strategic: when a corridor improvement is planned, that's the moment to acquire affordable units before prices spike. The linkage between infrastructure investment timing and ADCAP acquisition activity is the key policy innovation.

## ORGANIZATIONAL AND OPERATIONS

**Q: The 2020 merger was supposed to improve efficiency. Has it?**
A: The merger has had some success in reducing duplication between transportation planning and public works delivery. Capital project coordination has improved. However, vacancy rates in skilled trades and civil engineering remain a challenge. Cultural integration of two distinct departments took time. The full efficiency gains from the merger are still being realized.

**Q: Why are there so many vacancies? Can't the city hire faster?**
A: Civil service hiring in Texas municipalities involves competitive processes, salary benchmarks, and classification structures that make it difficult to compete with private sector wages for skilled engineers and trades workers. Austin has made some progress on classification and pay adjustments but remains behind market rates for some key roles. Delayed hiring compounds capital project delays.

**Q: Is TPW responsive to community input?**
A: TPW has robust formal public engagement processes for major capital projects. Community concerns about specific projects are often heard at the design stage. The equity gap is at the policy level, not the project level — there is no systematic mechanism for communities to influence which projects get funded, in what order, and with what anti-displacement protections attached. That's a Council-level policy gap, not a TPW operational gap.
