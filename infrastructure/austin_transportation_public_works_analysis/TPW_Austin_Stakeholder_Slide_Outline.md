### SLIDE 3 — TPW AT A GLANCE
**Visual: Department org chart / icon summary**

Key stats:
- Formed: 2020 (merger of Transportation + Public Works)
- Staff: ~1,100–1,300 FTEs
- Asset portfolio: 2,000+ lane miles, 100+ bridges, citywide drainage network
- Annual budget: ~$350–450M (all funds)
- Active bond program: $1.1B+

*Talking point: This is the department that fixes your roads, keeps you safe at crosswalks, and manages flooding during storms. It touches every resident every day.*

---

### SLIDE 4 — WHERE THE MONEY GOES
**Visual: Budget breakdown pie/bar chart (see exec02_policy_funding.csv)**

- General Fund (operations/maintenance): ~$120–140M
- Drainage Utility (stormwater): ~$80–100M
- Mobility Bond draws (capital): ~$75–100M
- Federal/state grants: ~$50–75M

*Talking point: Most of the budget is committed — bond programs are voter-approved, drainage fees are dedicated. The discretionary operational margin is thin.*

---

### SLIDE 5 — THE CAPITAL BACKLOG
**Visual: Bar chart — funded vs. unfunded need over time**

- Estimated backlog: **$3–5B+**
- Grows ~$100–200M/year
- Root cause: capital bonds fund new projects; maintenance appropriations don't keep pace with aging

*Talking point: We're building new while deferring maintenance on what we already have. Each bond cycle inherits a larger backlog.*

---

### SLIDE 6 — PAVEMENT: WHERE ARE THE GAPS?
**Visual: Map — PCI by neighborhood / heat map of low-PCI streets**

- Systemwide avg PCI: ~65 (target: 70+)
- Worst-performing streets: concentrated in East Austin, North Austin, older South Austin neighborhoods
- Correlation: lower PCI ↔ lower median income (r = 0.6–0.7 estimated)

*Talking point: The streets that need the most work are in the neighborhoods that can least afford the car repairs and safety risks that come with deteriorating pavement.*

---

### SLIDE 7 — SIDEWALK GAPS: 1,700–2,000 MILES
**Visual: Map — sidewalk gaps by census tract (darker = more gaps)**

- Gaps concentrated near: schools, transit stops, senior facilities, lower-income neighborhoods
- East Austin, North Austin, older inner-ring suburbs: highest gap density
- National equity data: sidewalk gaps correlate with pedestrian fatality risk

*Talking point: If you don't own a car, gaps in the sidewalk network are a public safety hazard. Many of these gaps are on or near the High Injury Network.*

---

### SLIDE 8 — VISION ZERO: THE HONEST ASSESSMENT
**Visual: Line chart — annual traffic fatalities 2016–2025 (see fig04_speed_kpis_sample.csv)**

- 2016 commitment: zero deaths by 2025
- Reality: ~100–120 deaths/year; no clear downward trend
- Pedestrian/cyclist deaths: 25–35% of total

*Talking point: We're doing the easy things — paint, signs, education. We're not doing the hard things — road redesign, speed reduction, structural safety investment. The results reflect that.*

---

### SLIDE 9 — HIGH INJURY NETWORK: WHERE AND WHO
**Visual: Map — HIN overlaid on income/race demographics**

Key HIN corridors:
- Rundberg Lane (N. Austin) — high poverty, high Hispanic population
- E. MLK Jr. / Airport Blvd (E. Austin) — historically Black/Hispanic corridor, gentrifying
- E. William Cannon (S. Austin) — lower-income residential
- N. Lamar (various segments)
- US 183 city-managed segments

*Talking point: 8% of streets, most of the deaths — and those streets run through Austin's most underserved communities. This is not a coincidence; it's the result of decades of road design that prioritized vehicle throughput over human safety.*

---

### SLIDE 10 — FLOOD RISK: FLASH FLOOD ALLEY
**Visual: Map — 100-year floodplain overlaid on income/race**

- Austin sits in Flash Flood Alley: steep limestone terrain + intense rain events
- Major at-risk watersheds: Onion Creek (SE), Williamson Creek (S/SW), Shoal Creek (Central), Walnut Creek (NE)
- 2013 Halloween Flood: 13 deaths on Onion Creek; triggered major buyout program
- Ongoing: new development in upper watersheds increasing downstream flood risk

*Talking point: Flash flooding is Austin's most dangerous natural hazard. And it disproportionately threatens the residents who can least afford to recover from it.*

---

### SLIDE 11 — DRAINAGE EQUITY
**Visual: Map — drainage infrastructure age / condition by neighborhood**

- Older drainage infrastructure concentrated in East and North Austin
- Drainage Utility fee structure: impervious cover-based; may burden small commercial and industrial properties in lower-income corridors
- Capital investment in drainage historically followed development patterns — newer, wealthier areas got newer systems

*Talking point: The drainage fee is supposed to be equitable because it's based on impervious cover. But the capital investment side — where the money actually goes — has historically favored newer, wealthier development areas.*

---

### SLIDE 12 — FLOOD BUYOUTS WITHOUT REPLACEMENT HOUSING
**Visual: Timeline — Onion Creek buyouts + displacement data**

- Onion Creek Buyout Program: voluntary acquisition in 100-year floodplain
- Hundreds of households relocated since 2013
- Problem: no systematic replacement housing program; many residents leave Austin entirely
- Result: flood mitigation success + permanent community displacement

*Talking point: We're protecting people from floods, but we're not ensuring they can stay in Austin. That's not a complete solution.*

---

### SLIDE 13 — INFRASTRUCTURE DRIVES DISPLACEMENT
**Visual: Before/after property value chart on corridor investment examples**

The cycle:
> **Bond investment → Corridor improvement → Amenity premium → Property value increase → Displacement**

Case examples:
- E. Cesar Chavez: street improvements preceded rapid gentrification
- Airport Blvd: active corridor investment, rising rents in adjacent blocks
- N. Lamar improvements: marketed as amenity in luxury real estate listings

*Talking point: Public investment is not equity-neutral. Every dollar we put into a corridor in East or North Austin — without affordability protections — is potentially funding displacement.*

---

### SLIDE 14 — THE POLICY GAP
**Visual: Simple 2x2 — infrastructure investment vs. affordability protection**

Currently:
- Many corridors: **High investment, Low protection** → displacement risk
- Some corridors: **Low investment, Low protection** → disinvestment + future displacement risk

Goal:
- All equity-priority corridors: **High investment + High protection** → shared prosperity

*Talking point: Austin has the tools to do both — TPW's capital program plus the ADCAP framework for affordability preservation. What's missing is the policy linkage.*

---

### SLIDE 15 — RECOMMENDATIONS
**Visual: Priority matrix (impact vs. effort)**

1. **Equity-in-Infrastructure Framework** — disaggregated service standards; equity progress report annually
2. **Displacement Impact Review** — required for all capital projects >$5M in equity-priority areas
3. **HIN Corridor Redesign** — 2+ full structural redesigns/year; fatality data disaggregated by income
4. **Drainage Equity Audit** — capital distribution and fee structure review
5. **Infrastructure-Affordability Linkage** — joint TPW + Housing directive; pair corridor investment with ADCAP tools
6. **Next Bond Cycle Equity Targeting** — explicit equity allocation in next mobility bond

---

### SLIDE 16 — CALL TO ACTION
**What Council can do today:**

- Direct TPW to develop Equity-in-Infrastructure framework (no budget required)
- Add Displacement Impact Review to capital project approval checklist
- Request joint briefing from TPW + Housing on infrastructure-affordability linkage
- Require equity-disaggregated reporting in next TPW performance report

*Talking point: None of these require new money — they require new direction. The tools exist. The resources are partially in place. What's needed is Council will.*
