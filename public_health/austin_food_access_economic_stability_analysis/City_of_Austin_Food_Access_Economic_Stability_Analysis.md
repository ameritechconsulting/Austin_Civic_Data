# City of Austin: Food Access & Economic Stability Analysis

## Executive Summary

Food access and economic stability are deeply connected in the City of Austin. This report builds an independent analysis of Austin’s current landscape, focusing on: SNAP participation and food insecurity, grocery retail geography and food desert mapping, wage versus cost-of-living pressures, and childcare affordability. The analysis also centers equity, identifying neighborhoods and demographic groups experiencing disproportionate hardship.

Austin has strong economic growth, but the benefits are unevenly distributed. Low-income households and families of color continue to face food insecurity, lengthy grocery trips, and a wage gap that undermines basic livability. Childcare costs further reduce family budgets and limit workforce participation.

This five-page summary offers an actionable overview of the evidence, the dimensions of the challenge, and targeted recommendations for local partners, policymakers, and practitioners.

## 1. Austin Today: Economic and Social Landscape

### 1.1 Market Transformation, Growth, and Dislocation

Austin’s rapid growth has reshaped the city. High-tech expansion and population inflows have driven housing demand, increased commercial investment, and lifted some wages. Yet the same growth has also made Austin one of the most expensive cities in Texas for housing, childcare, and transportation.

Key trends in Austin’s current environment:

- Population growth has accelerated land and rental prices, particularly in central and east Austin.
- Wage gains are uneven; higher-income households benefit more than lower-paid workers in food service, retail, and caregiving.
- Public transit and walkability are growing but still lag behind the needs of low-income neighborhoods.

### 1.2 Food Insecurity as a Persistent Local Issue

Food insecurity in Austin is not a distant or rare issue. It exists in neighborhoods that are also experiencing rapid gentrification and displacement. Austin Public Health and community organizations report that many families still struggle to access affordable, nutritious food.

Food insecurity is often masked by visible prosperity, but depths of household hardship remain in the following forms:

- Increased demand for food pantry and meal programs
- Elevated SNAP participation in targeted ZIP codes
- Concentrated child food insecurity in East and South Austin

### 1.3 Equity Context

Austin’s economic growth has widened racial and geographic disparities. Families of color, immigrant households, and single-parent families face multiple economic burdens in parallel:

- Lower median incomes for Black and Hispanic households
- Higher rates of child poverty and rent burden
- Fewer long-term investments in healthy food retail and community services in low-income neighborhoods

A complete analysis of food access and economic stability must therefore incorporate equity metrics, not only average citywide outcomes.

## 2. SNAP Usage and Food Insecurity

### 2.1 Understanding SNAP as a Hardship Indicator

SNAP participation is a strong indicator of food insecurity in a community. Households receiving SNAP benefits typically have incomes below state thresholds and may struggle with fluctuating monthly budgets.

For Austin, relevant SNAP data points include:

- Enrollment by ZIP code and census tract
- Changes in participation rates over time
- Association with child poverty, unemployment, and housing instability

### 2.2 Spatial Patterns of SNAP Participation

Data suggests that high SNAP participation is concentrated in areas with historic disinvestment. These areas often correspond with:

- East Austin neighborhoods east of I-35
- South Austin communities near South Congress and Brodie Lane
- West and North Austin pockets with high affordability pressure and transit dependence

These spatial patterns matter because they align with food access gaps and limited local retail options.

### 2.3 Correlation with Household Poverty and Child Food Insecurity

Domestic food insecurity is typically highest in households with:

- Children under age 18
- Single parents or single adult households
- Low-wage jobs with irregular hours

In Austin, children are often overrepresented among SNAP households. This means families with school-age children experience both food hardship and rising costs for childcare, rent, and transportation.

### 2.4 SNAP Trends and Program Challenges

A few important observations about SNAP in Austin:

- SNAP remains underutilized by some eligible populations, especially immigrant and mixed-status households.
- Access barriers include stigma, documentation requirements, and lack of enrollment support in underserved neighborhoods.
- Policy changes at the state and federal levels can influence the size and stability of SNAP benefits, affecting local hunger relief.

## 3. Grocery Store Access and Food Desert Mapping

### 3.1 Defining Food Deserts and Low-Access Zones

Food deserts are neighborhoods where residents have limited access to affordable and nutritious food. In Austin, food access is often defined by the following characteristics:

- Distance greater than one mile to a full-service grocery store
- Low vehicle ownership combined with limited public transit service
- Low-income status and high SNAP participation

These geographic conditions create ‘low income, low access’ (LILA) zones, which require targeted intervention.

### 3.2 Grocery Retail Landscape in Austin

Austin’s grocery landscape contains a mix of national chains, discount stores, ethnic markets, and small convenience retailers. Important zoning facts include:

- Full-service supermarkets are more common in central, north, and west Austin.
- East and South Austin have fewer large grocery stores and more small convenience or dollar stores.
- Some underserved neighborhoods have community markets or mobile food programs, but not enough to close the access gap.

### 3.3 Mapping Access by Zip Code and Transit

The spatial gap is not only about distance; it is also about the mode of travel. Many households in target areas rely on transit, walking, or rideshares to reach grocery stores. Key implications:

- Food access is especially limited for households without cars.
- Transit routes do not always align with grocery retail locations.
- Grocery trips are time-consuming and expensive when residents must cross major highways or travel large distances.

### 3.4 Equity Dimension of Food Retail Distribution

A core equity finding is that grocery retail investment is uneven. Neighborhoods with greater concentrations of Black and Hispanic residents have:

- Lower store counts per capita
- Fewer stores offering fresh produce
- Higher prevalence of unhealthy convenience retail

This means grocery deserts are not simply a matter of geography; they are also a matter of racially differentiated investment.

## 4. Wage vs Cost-of-Living Pressures

### 4.1 Austin’s Cost-of-Living Landscape

Austin’s cost-of-living has grown across multiple categories:

- Housing: rental and purchase prices are high and rising faster than many incomes.
- Food: grocery inflation, especially for fresh fruits, vegetables, and protein, outpaces general inflation.
- Childcare: monthly costs for infant and preschool care are substantial.
- Transportation: auto ownership and transit costs add pressure for low-income households.

When these factors combine, many households are squeezed into a narrow budget band.

### 4.2 Wage Distribution and Living Wage Gap

The living wage for a single adult in Austin is substantially higher than the federal minimum salary. For family households, the gap is wider:

- Service workers, retail employees, and hospitality staff are often paid below the living wage.
- Part-time and gig workers face greater instability and fewer benefits.

The result is that many working households still qualify for SNAP and other assistance despite having employment.

### 4.3 Rent Burden and Food Choices

Housing costs are the largest share of household budgets. Rent burden is defined as spending more than 30% of income on housing. In Austin:

- Low-income households are more likely to be rent-burdened.
- Rent burden often forces households to reduce spending on food, healthcare, and childcare.

This tradeoff can make it difficult for families to choose healthier food, which is frequently more expensive and less available in their neighborhoods.

### 4.4 Childcare Costs as a Second Rent

Childcare is often referred to as a second rent because of its high cost. In Austin:

- Monthly childcare for infants and toddlers can rival rent payments.
- Subsidy eligibility is limited and may not cover all families in need.
- Parents who cannot afford childcare are less likely to work full-time, limiting earnings.

When childcare costs are included in the household budget, the gap between wages and required income grows significantly.

## 5. Childcare and Workforce Participation

### 5.1 Childcare as Economic Infrastructure

Childcare is a critical part of economic infrastructure. For parents to participate fully in the labor market, reliable and affordable childcare is essential.

In Austin, the childcare system faces several pressures:

- Supply shortages for licensed childcare providers
- High operating costs for providers relative to revenue
- Workforce shortages within the childcare workforce itself

### 5.2 Impact on Women and Working Families

Childcare affordability is especially significant for mothers. It affects:

- Workforce participation rates among women
- Child poverty and long-term family economic mobility
- Career advancement opportunities for parents in low-wage jobs

### 5.3 Childcare Burden and Food Security

There is a direct link between childcare burden and food security. Households paying more for childcare often have less discretionary income for groceries. In some cases, families must choose between childcare and food or reduce the quality of their diet.

### 5.4 Policy Levers for Childcare Support

Potential policy responses include:

- Expanding subsidy programs for working families
- Supporting employer-based childcare options
- Investing in community-based early childhood centers in underserved areas
- Aligning childcare funding with neighborhood-level need and food insecurity metrics

## 6. Equity Lens: Who is Most Impacted?

### 6.1 Race, Ethnicity, and Income

Austin’s racial equity dynamics matter for food access and economic stability. The most impacted groups include:

- Black households, who face higher poverty and lower median incomes
- Hispanic households, who often live in neighborhoods with fewer grocery options and higher transit dependence
- Native and immigrant families, who may have restricted access to SNAP and other assistance

### 6.2 Neighborhood-Level Inequities

Certain neighborhoods are excellent examples of intersecting hardship. These include:

- East Austin: high SNAP participation, fewer supermarkets, and rapid displacement pressure
- South Austin: low-income neighborhoods with limited retail and childcare resources
- Central Austin fringes: areas where rising housing costs push low-income families farther from jobs and grocery stores

### 6.3 Children and Multi-Generational Families

Children and multi-generational households experience economic stress differently:

- Children in food-insecure households are more likely to have poor nutrition and academic challenges.
- Multi-generational families may share resources, but they also face higher overall expenses.

This means policies must be calibrated for both immediate food relief and long-term family stability.

## 7. Priority Opportunity Areas

### 7.1 East Austin Food Access Hotspots

East Austin includes census tracts that combine high poverty, high SNAP usage, and low supermarket access. Interventions here could include:

- Incentivizing full-service grocery development
- Expanding farmers market and mobile market access
- Supporting nonprofit food distribution hubs

### 7.2 South Austin and High-Need ZIP Codes

ZIP codes such as 78723, 78744, and 78756 should be prioritized because they demonstrate:

- Elevated SNAP participation and child poverty
- Low grocery store density per capita
- Greater reliance on transit for food shopping

### 7.3 Transit-Dependent Food Access Corridors

Food access is also an infrastructure issue. Key corridors with poor transit-aligned grocery access should be prioritized for:

- Improved bus service to grocery destinations
- Grocery shuttle or ride-share subsidy programs
- Last-mile connections for families without vehicles

## 8. Recommendations for Austin

### 8.1 Build a Food Access Investment Plan

- Create an Austin Food Access Fund to support supermarkets, mobile markets, healthy convenience retail, and community kitchens.
- Prioritize funding based on overlapping indicators: SNAP rate, LILA status, rent burden, and childcare need.
- Use a community-led scoring process to ensure investments align with neighborhood priorities.

### 8.2 Strengthen SNAP Outreach and Enrollment

- Increase SNAP outreach in schools, health clinics, and community centers.
- Provide enrollment assistance in Spanish and other languages.
- Partner with community organizations to reduce stigma and streamline application support.

### 8.3 Expand Childcare Supports as Economic Relief

- Invest in childcare subsidies targeted to families in high SNAP and rent-burdened neighborhoods.
- Back childcare workforce development, including training and wage supports for providers.
- Encourage employers to offer flexible schedules and childcare benefits.

### 8.4 Align Wage Policy with Housing and Food Costs

- Track a local Austin living wage benchmark and compare it against median earnings in key sectors.
- Promote living wage requirements for city contractors and service vendors.
- Support training and career pathways in higher-demand, better-paying industries.

### 8.5 Use Data-Driven Equity Metrics

- Develop an Austin Food Equity Dashboard with tract-level indicators for SNAP, store access, poverty, race, and childcare burden.
- Update metrics annually and use them in budget, planning, and grant decisions.
- Ensure community input is embedded in data selection and interpretation.

## 9. Data and Methodology Appendix

### 9.1 Data Sources

This analysis is based on publicly available data sources that are particularly relevant to Austin:

- City of Austin open data (grocery store locations, food access, SNAP sites)
- U.S. Census American Community Survey (ACS)
- USDA Food Access Research Atlas
- Texas Workforce Commission and childcare cost reports
- Austin Public Health community health data

### 9.2 Analytical Framework

The report uses a multi-layered analytical framework:

1. Economic vulnerability: SNAP usage, poverty, rent burden
2. Physical access: grocery store proximity, transit access, vehicle ownership
3. Cost pressure: wages, living wage gap, childcare costs
4. Equity overlay: race, ethnicity, family composition, neighborhood history

### 9.3 Recommended Metrics

Key metrics for ongoing monitoring and program design include:

- SNAP participation rate by tract and ZIP code
- Share of population living more than one mile from a full-service grocery store
- Grocery store count per 10,000 residents
- Childcare cost as a share of median household income
- Share of households spending more than 30% of income on rent
- Racial/ethnic composition of targeted food access neighborhoods

## 10. Conclusion

Austin has a significant opportunity to align growth with inclusion. Food access and economic stability are intertwined: improving one improves the other.

This five-page analysis demonstrates that:

- Food insecurity in Austin remains concentrated in identifiable neighborhoods and vulnerable populations.
- Grocery access is uneven and often correlated with both income and race.
- Wage gaps and high childcare costs are major factors limiting family stability.
- An equity-centered approach can target resources where they will have the greatest effect.

The recommendations point to a coordinated strategy of food retail investment, SNAP outreach, childcare support, and wage policy. With stronger data, targeted programs, and community leadership, Austin can reduce food insecurity and strengthen economic stability for its most impacted residents.
