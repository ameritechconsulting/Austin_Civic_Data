# PAGE 1 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Austin is one of the fastest-growing cities in the United States. Its economic expansion has generated wealth, jobs, and national recognition — yet that prosperity has not reached all residents equally. For lower-income households, particularly those in eastern and southeastern zip codes and among communities of color, three compounding forces are undermining food security and economic stability:

1. **Persistent food insecurity** concentrated in specific geographies
2. **A structural mismatch** between wages and the real cost of living
3. **Childcare costs** that price families — especially mothers — out of the workforce

These are not isolated problems. They reinforce each other. A family without reliable grocery access, earning below living wage, and unable to afford childcare faces a compounding deficit that no single program can resolve. Effective policy must address all three simultaneously.

---

---

## Why This Matters Now

Austin's growth is pricing out the residents who built it. Between 2015 and 2024, median home values in Travis County rose approximately 85% while real wages for the bottom quartile grew less than 12%. The consequence is not just individual hardship — it is a structural erosion of the workforce, the tax base, and the community fabric that supports long-term city vitality.

The families most affected are disproportionately Black and Hispanic/Latino, in communities whose geographic disadvantage was deliberately constructed through the City's own 1928 segregation plan. Addressing these inequities is not charity; it is the correction of a policy failure.

# PAGE 2 — FOOD INSECURITY & SNAP PARTICIPATION

## Who Goes Hungry — and Where

**Feeding America's 2024 Map the Meal Gap** estimates that 14.8% of Travis County residents — approximately 145,000 individuals — are food insecure. This rate has remained above the national average (12.8%) despite the county's overall income growth, revealing the distributional failure of Austin's economic boom.

Children bear disproportionate risk. The **child food insecurity rate in Travis County is estimated at 19.2%**, compared to 13.4% nationally. For working families whose earnings are already stretched by housing and childcare, food is often the most flexible expense — and the first to be cut.

---

## SNAP Participation — A Geography of Need

SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) enrollment is the most reliable zip-code-level proxy for food hardship. The data reveal a stark divide:

### Highest-Need Zip Codes

|---|---|---|---|
| **78753** | Rundberg / N. Lamar | **42%** | $34,100 |

| **78724** | Colony Park / E. Austin | **35%** | $39,500 |
| **78721** | E. 51st / Govalle | **33%** | $41,200 |

### Lowest-Need Zip Codes

| Zip Code | Neighborhood | SNAP Rate | Median HH Income |
|---|---|---|---|

| 78733 | Bee Cave / W. Lake Hills | 2% | $149,200 |

**The gap between Austin's highest- and lowest-SNAP zip codes is 40 percentage points.** This is not a reflection of different values or behaviors — it is the direct outcome of decades of underinvestment in eastern Austin.

---

## Hidden Need: The Enrollment Gap

SNAP enrollment undercounts actual need by an estimated 30–40%. Eligible households that do not enroll cite:

- **Stigma** and social barriers to application
- **Chilling effects** on mixed-status immigrant households following public charge policy changes
- **Application complexity** and inability to take time off work to complete enrollment
- **Language barriers** in the enrollment process

In the five highest-SNAP zip codes alone, an estimated **11,973 additional eligible households** are not enrolled, representing a potential 37% expansion of unmet need above current enrollment figures.

# PAGE 3 — GROCERY ACCESS & FOOD DESERTS

## The Geography of Nutritional Deprivation

The USDA Economic Research Service designates a **food desert** as a low-income census tract where a significant share of residents lives more than 1 mile from the nearest supermarket. In Travis County, four zip codes fully meet this criteria and three more are partially designated — together housing approximately **116,000 residents**.

### Food Desert Zip Code Summary

| Zip | Neighborhood | Status | Miles to Grocery | Supermarkets ≤1 mi | Dollar Stores ≤1 mi | % No Vehicle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **78725** | Hornsby Bend | Full Desert | **4.1 mi** | 0 | 1 | 19.3% |
| **78724** | Colony Park | Full Desert | 2.3 mi | 0 | 3 | 28.1% |
| **78742** | Montopolis | Full Desert | 1.9 mi | 0 | 2 | **31.4%** |
| **78721** | E. 51st / Govalle | Full Desert | 1.6 mi | 0 | 2 | 26.8% |
| **78753** | Rundberg | Partial | 1.1 mi | 1 | 4 | 22.4% |
| **78741** | Riverside | Partial | 1.2 mi | 1 | 3 | 24.7% |
| **78744** | Far SE Austin | Partial | 1.8 mi | 0 | 2 | 18.6% |

---

## Three Compounding Access Barriers

**1. Distance without transit.** Hornsby Bend (78725) has no supermarket within 4 miles and no Cap Metro service connecting residents to grocery anchors. Colony Park (78724) has no direct bus route to the nearest HEB despite 28% of households having no vehicle.

**2. The dollar store substitution problem.** In full food desert zip codes, dollar stores outnumber supermarkets 3-to-1. Dollar stores stock minimal fresh produce, and per-unit pricing on staple goods is frequently higher than supermarkets. Their proliferation in food-insecure areas functions as nutritional dead weight — present, but not nourishing.

**3. The gentrification paradox in East Austin.** Some historically food-insecure zip codes (notably 78702 East Austin) now show improved grocery access on paper due to gentrification-driven retail investment. However, the new stores (Whole Foods, specialty grocers) are priced for incoming demographics, not legacy low-income residents — many of whom have been displaced to zip codes with worse access. Raw access metrics can overstate equity improvements when affordability is not considered.

---

## What Adequate Access Looks Like — and Doesn't

Zip codes in western and northwestern Austin (78703, 78759, 78733) average 0.4–0.6 miles to the nearest supermarket, with 2–3 options within 1 mile, near-zero dollar store density, and healthy food scores of 8.9–9.1 out of 10. The contrast with 78725's 2.4/10 score and 4.1-mile grocery gap is not incidental — it reflects who the city has historically invested in.

# PAGE 4 — WAGES, COST OF LIVING & CHILDCARE

## The Living Wage Gap

Using **MIT Living Wage Calculator** methodology for the Austin-Round Rock MSA, the annual income required to meet basic needs varies dramatically by household composition — and Austin's median wage fails to meet it for every household type with children.

### Living Wage vs. Actual Earnings

| Household Type | Living Wage Needed | Austin Median Wage | Annual Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Adult, No Children | $46,884 | $47,320 | **+$436** |
| **1 Adult, 1 Child** | **$79,248** | **$47,320** | **-$31,928** |
| 1 Adult, 2 Children | $99,372 | $47,320 | **-$52,052** |
| 2 Adults, No Children | $62,880 | $94,640 | +$31,760 |
| 2 Adults, 1 Child | $93,264 | $94,640 | +$1,376 |
| **2 Adults, 2 Children** | **$112,080** | **$94,640** | **-$17,440** |

The single adult without children is the only household type that approaches living wage adequacy at median earnings. A single parent with one child — among the most common family structures in high-SNAP zip codes — faces a structural annual deficit of nearly **$32,000**. This gap cannot be closed through wage gains alone without simultaneously addressing childcare and housing costs.

**Texas minimum wage** remains $7.25/hour (unchanged since 2009), yielding $15,080 annually — 19% of the living wage needed by a single parent with one child. Approximately **21% of private-sector workers** in Travis County earn below $15/hour.

---

## Housing: The Largest Cost Driver

- Median 2-bedroom rent in Austin: **~$1,900/month** (2024)
- Families in 78753 and 78741 spend an estimated **58–62% of gross income on rent alone**
- HUD defines cost-burdened as >30% of income; **41% of Austin renter households** meet this threshold
- At 58–62% of income on housing, families have $13,000–15,000 annually for all other necessities — food, childcare, transportation, healthcare

---

## Childcare: The Hidden Workforce Tax

Austin's childcare market is among the most expensive in Texas:

| Care Type | Monthly Cost | Annual Cost | % of Median Family Income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infant (center-based) | **$1,847** | **$22,164** | **29.6%** |
| Toddler (center-based) | $1,612 | $19,344 | 25.8% |
| Preschool (3–5 yrs) | $1,388 | $16,656 | 22.2% |
| School-Age | $924 | $11,088 | 14.8% |

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services defines childcare as **affordable at 7% or less of family income** (~$5,250/year at the Travis County median). Infant care costs **4.2 times** that threshold.

### Direct Workforce Suppression Effects

| Impact | Share of Parents |
|---|---|
| Paying more than 10% of income on childcare | 41.3% |
| Reduced work hours due to childcare cost | 28.7% |
| Turned down a job offer (childcare would eliminate net gain) | 19.6% |
| **Left the workforce entirely** | **14.2%** |
| Mothers vs. fathers who exit workforce for childcare | **4.8x more likely** |

Travis County has a licensed childcare **slot gap of 26,400** (89,200 children needing care vs. 62,800 licensed slots). The average wait for a childcare subsidy (CCMS) is **18 months**, with 9,800 families currently on the waitlist. The Austin Chamber of Commerce estimates the regional childcare gap costs the metro economy **$1.2 billion annually** in lost productivity and reduced labor supply.

# PAGE 5 — EQUITY ANALYSIS & POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

## The Burden Is Not Equally Shared

Food insecurity, grocery access gaps, and economic instability are not randomly distributed across Austin. They are concentrated along racial lines — lines drawn in large part by historical policy.

### Food Insecurity and Economic Access by Race/Ethnicity

| Group | % of County Pop. | Food Insecurity Rate | % in Food Desert Zip | Childcare Burden (% income) | Median HH Income |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| White Non-Hispanic | 48.2% | 10.2% | 8.4% | 14.1% | $98,400 |
| **Hispanic / Latino** | **26.4%** | **21.8%** | **42.6%** | **31.4%** | $52,300 |
| **Black / African American** | **8.1%** | **19.6%** | **51.3%** | **34.2%** | $48,700 |
| Asian / Pacific Islander | 7.3% | 9.8% | 12.4% | 16.8% | $87,600 |
| Multiracial | 4.8% | 14.2% | 22.7% | 22.1% | $63,400 |

**Black residents are 1.9x more likely** to live in a food desert zip code than the county average. **Hispanic/Latino residents face a food insecurity rate more than double** that of White Non-Hispanic residents. These disparities are structurally produced, not individually caused.

---

## The Historical Root

Austin's 1928 City Master Plan explicitly relocated Black residents east of Chicon Street and concentrated city services in that corridor to enforce racial segregation. Grocery investment, transit routes, park amenities, and school quality all followed — and reinforced — those boundaries. The zip codes with the highest SNAP rates and worst grocery access today map almost precisely onto the boundaries drawn in 1928. **Any serious food access equity strategy must name and address this history directly.**

---

## Intersectionality: Where All Three Crises Meet

An estimated **18,000–22,000 households** in Travis County simultaneously face:
- Living in a food desert zip code
- Earning below the living wage
- No access to childcare subsidies

These households are overwhelmingly Black and Hispanic/Latino, concentrated in the eastern crescent of the city. For these families, food, economic stability, and childcare are not three problems — they are one.

---

## Policy Recommendations

### Food Access — Immediate (0–12 months)
- **Expand mobile food pantry routes** in 78724, 78725, 78742, and 78721 using City fleet or Central Texas Food Bank partnerships
- **Fund SNAP outreach workers** in eastern zip codes co-located with WIC offices, public schools, and community health clinics for co-enrollment
- **Adopt a formal City policy** restricting new dollar-store-only food retail in food desert census tracts (precedent: Pittsburgh, PA)

### Food Access — Medium-Term (1–3 years)
- **Use economic development incentives** (tax abatements, infrastructure) to attract full-service grocery to Colony Park and Montopolis
- **Redesign Cap Metro routes** to improve east-to-grocery connectivity, prioritizing transit from 78724, 78742, and 78721 to existing supermarket anchors
- **Fund community or cooperative grocery models** in deep food deserts where commercial retail is unlikely to enter without subsidy

### Economic Stability — Immediate
- **Extend living wage ordinance** to cover all City contractors, building on Austin's existing $22/hr floor for direct municipal employees
- **Expand Emergency Rental Assistance** programs; current funding meets a fraction of the scale of severely cost-burdened households

### Economic Stability — Medium-Term
- **Tie employer living wage commitments** to economic development incentives for all major commercial developments (>100 employees or significant tax benefit)
- **Support state-level minimum wage legislation**; coordinate Austin, Harris County, and Dallas delegations for a Texas-wide push above the $7.25/hr floor

### Childcare — Immediate
- **Increase City investment in the Child Care Relief Fund** to add 2,000 subsidized slots in Year 1, directly reducing the CCMS waitlist backlog
- **Require childcare needs assessments** as part of all major development permit reviews (>200 units or >50,000 sq ft commercial)

### Childcare — Medium-Term
- **Develop a City-anchored early learning center** in Colony Park or a comparable eastern neighborhood as a proof-of-concept for municipal childcare infrastructure
- **Incentivize employer-sponsored childcare** through City economic development agreements — employers providing on-site or near-site care receive enhanced permitting or tax treatment
- **Advocate for federal CDCTC expansion** and for Texas to adopt a state-level supplement to help families below the CCDF subsidy threshold

---

## Conclusion

Austin can choose the kind of city it becomes. The data in this analysis do not describe an inevitable outcome — they describe a correctable one. The zip codes most harmed by food insecurity, wage gaps, and childcare costs are also those most likely to respond to targeted, sustained investment. The cost of action is measurable. The cost of inaction — in public health, workforce capacity, and generational poverty — is far higher.
