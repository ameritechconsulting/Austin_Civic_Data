## Executive Summary

Austin is one of the fastest-growing cities in the United States, yet the prosperity of its economic boom has not reached all residents equally. This analysis examines three compounding forces that undermine food security and economic stability for lower-income Austin households: persistent food insecurity, a structural mismatch between wages and the real cost of living, and childcare costs that effectively price families—especially mothers—out of the workforce.

**Key Findings:**

| Finding | Metric |
|---|---|
| Travis County residents experiencing food insecurity | ~14.8% (~145,000 people) |
| SNAP households in Travis County | ~95,000 |
| Zip codes meeting federal food desert criteria | 4 designated; 3 partial |
| Wage gap for a single parent with 1 child | **-$31,928/year** below living wage |
| Annual infant childcare cost vs. HHS affordability threshold | **$22,164 vs. $5,250** |
| Workers who left the workforce due to childcare costs | 14.2% of surveyed parents |
| Estimated annual economic output lost to childcare gap | **$1.2 billion** (Austin metro) |

The data reveal a compounding inequality: the zip codes with the highest SNAP usage (78753, 78741, 78744) are also those with the greatest grocery store access gaps and the highest concentrations of households of color. A single parent earning Austin's median wage faces a structural annual shortfall of nearly $32,000 relative to the true cost of living.

## 1. Food Insecurity Landscape

### 1.1 Scale of the Problem

Feeding America's 2024 Map the Meal Gap estimates that **14.8% of Travis County residents** are food insecure—approximately 145,000 individuals. This figure has remained stubbornly above the national average (12.8%) despite Travis County's overall income growth, reflecting the distributional effects of Austin's economic expansion.

Food insecurity in Austin is not uniformly distributed:
- Residents in eastern and southeastern zip codes (78724, 78741, 78744, 78753) experience food insecurity rates **2–3x higher** than those in western and northwestern zip codes.
- Children face disproportionate risk: the child food insecurity rate in Travis County is estimated at **19.2%**, compared to 13.4% nationally.

### 1.2 SNAP Participation by Zip Code

SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) data provide a direct proxy for household-level food insecurity. The table below summarizes the highest-need zip codes in Austin:

| Zip Code | Neighborhood | SNAP Households | SNAP % of HH | Median HH Income |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 78753 | Rundberg / N. Lamar | 8,484 | **42%** | $34,100 |
| 78741 | Riverside / SE Austin | 7,182 | **38%** | $36,800 |
| 78744 | Far SE Austin | 6,696 | **36%** | $38,200 |
| 78724 | Colony Park / E. Austin | 5,880 | **35%** | $39,500 |
| 78721 | E. 51st / Govalle | 4,191 | **33%** | $41,200 |

In contrast, affluent zip codes (78703 Tarrytown, 78733 Bee Cave/W. Lake Hills) report SNAP participation rates of 2%, with median household incomes exceeding $138,000.

**The SNAP gap between Austin's wealthiest and most disadvantaged zip codes is 40 percentage points.**

### 1.3 Barriers Beyond Eligibility

SNAP enrollment does not capture the full picture. Research consistently shows that 30–40% of eligible households do not participate due to:
- Stigma and application complexity
- Mixed-status immigrant family fears (chilling effect of public charge rules)
- Inability to take time off work to complete enrollment
- Language barriers in application processes

This suggests the true unmet need in Austin's high-SNAP-usage zip codes is meaningfully larger than reported enrollment figures.

## 2. Grocery Store Access and Food Deserts

### 2.1 USDA Food Desert Designations in Austin

The USDA Economic Research Service defines a **food desert** as a low-income census tract where a significant share of residents is more than 1 mile (urban) or 10 miles (rural) from the nearest supermarket. In Travis County:

- **4 zip codes** fully meet food desert criteria: 78724, 78725, 78742, 78721
- **3 additional zip codes** are classified as partial food deserts: 78753, 78741, 78744
- These 7 zip codes together house approximately **116,000 residents**

### 2.2 The Dollar Store vs. Supermarket Disparity

One of the most striking patterns in Austin's food environment is the inverse relationship between supermarket access and dollar store concentration. In zip codes with the lowest healthy food scores:

| Zip Code | Supermarkets ≤1 mi | Dollar Stores ≤1 mi | Healthy Food Score (1–10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 78725 | 0 | 1 | 2.4 |
| 78724 | 0 | 3 | 3.1 |
| 78742 | 0 | 2 | 3.4 |
| 78721 | 0 | 2 | 4.1 |

Dollar stores stock limited fresh produce and often price-per-unit is higher than supermarkets for staple goods. Their proliferation in food-insecure areas functions as a form of food environment substitution that does not meet nutritional access standards.

### 2.3 Transportation and Vehicle Access as a Multiplier

The intersection of food desert geography and lack of vehicle access creates compounding hardship. In 78742 (Montopolis), **31.4% of households have no vehicle**—the highest rate in the city—while the nearest supermarket is 1.9 miles away. Austin's transit network (Cap Metro) does not provide frequent or direct service between many eastern residential clusters and grocery anchors.

**Key access gaps:**
- Colony Park (78724): No direct bus route to nearest HEB; 28.1% no-vehicle households
- Hornsby Bend (78725): Rural fringe; 4.1 miles to nearest supermarket; no transit
- Montopolis (78742): River geography creates physical barrier to eastern grocery options

### 2.4 The Gentrification Paradox in East Austin

A counterintuitive finding: some zip codes in East Austin (78702) that historically qualified as food deserts now have improved retail grocery access due to gentrification-driven development. However, the new stores (Whole Foods Market, specialty grocers) are priced for the incoming demographic, not the legacy low-income residents—who in many cases have been displaced to zip codes with worse access. This phenomenon, sometimes called **"green gentrification"** in food access research, means raw access metrics can overstate equity improvements.

## 3. Wage vs. Cost of Living

### 3.1 Austin's Living Wage Reality

Using the MIT Living Wage Calculator methodology, the living wage required to meet basic needs in the Austin-Round Rock MSA varies significantly by household composition:

| Household Type | Annual Living Wage Needed | Annual Median Wage (Actual) | Annual Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Adult, No Children | $46,884 | $47,320 | +$436 |
| **1 Adult, 1 Child** | **$79,248** | **$47,320** | **-$31,928** |
| 1 Adult, 2 Children | $99,372 | $47,320 | -$52,052 |
| 2 Adults, 2 Children | $112,080 | $94,640 | -$17,440 |

The single adult without children is the only household type that approaches living wage adequacy at median earnings. Any household with children—and especially single-parent households—faces a structural deficit that cannot be closed through individual wage gains alone without addressing childcare costs simultaneously.

### 3.2 Housing Cost Pressure

Austin's housing costs have risen dramatically over the last decade:
- Median rent for a 2-bedroom unit: **~$1,800–2,100/month** (2024)
- Homeownership median price: **~$485,000** (2024, down from $600K peak but still historically high)
- HUD-defined housing cost burden (>30% of income): affects an estimated **41%** of Austin renter households

Low-income renters in the highest-SNAP zip codes typically spend **50–65% of their gross income on housing**, leaving insufficient resources for food, childcare, transportation, and healthcare—a phenomenon economists call "residual income poverty."

### 3.3 The Minimum Wage Floor

Texas has not increased its minimum wage above the federal floor of **$7.25/hour** since 2009. At full-time hours (2,080 hours/year), the minimum wage yields $15,080 annually—**32% of the living wage for a single parent with one child**. Even for a single adult without children, minimum wage earnings cover only 32% of living costs.

The City of Austin itself raised its internal minimum wage to $22/hour for city employees, acknowledging that the state floor is insufficient. However, approximately **21% of private-sector workers** in Travis County earn below $15/hour.

## 4. Childcare: The Hidden Economic Barrier

### 4.1 Cost Burden

Austin's childcare market is among the most expensive in Texas and significantly above national averages:

| Care Type | Monthly Cost | Annual Cost | % of Median Family Income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infant (center-based) | $1,847 | $22,164 | **29.6%** |
| Toddler (center-based) | $1,612 | $19,344 | **25.8%** |
| Preschool (3–5 yrs) | $1,388 | $16,656 | **22.2%** |
| School-Age Care | $924 | $11,088 | **14.8%** |

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services defines childcare as affordable at **7% or less of family income**. For a family earning the Travis County median (~$75,000/year), this threshold is $5,250/year—**less than 1/4 of the actual cost of infant care**.

### 4.2 Workforce Participation Effects

The childcare cost crisis directly suppresses workforce participation:

- **14.2%** of Austin-area parents left the workforce entirely due to childcare costs
- **28.7%** reduced their work hours
- **19.6%** turned down a job offer because childcare would eliminate net income gains
- Mothers are **4.8x more likely** than fathers to exit the workforce for childcare reasons

This is not simply a personal economic hardship—it represents a structural suppression of labor supply in a metro area already experiencing workforce shortages in key sectors (healthcare, education, hospitality, construction).

### 4.3 Supply Gap

Travis County faces a significant licensed childcare slot deficit:
- Children under 6 needing care: **89,200**
- Licensed slots available: **62,800**
- **Gap: 26,400 slots** (approximately 30% of demand unmet by licensed providers)

Families on the Texas Child Care and Development Fund (CCDF/CCMS) subsidy waitlist in Travis County wait an average of **18 months** for assistance. As of 2024, approximately **9,800 families** are on that waitlist.

### 4.4 Economic Output Impact

The Austin Chamber of Commerce estimates that the regional childcare gap costs the Austin metropolitan economy approximately **$1.2 billion annually** in lost productivity, worker turnover, and reduced labor force participation—a cost borne largely by employers, workers, and public systems rather than distributed across the full economy.

## 5. Equity Analysis

### 5.1 Disproportionate Impacts by Race and Ethnicity

The distribution of food insecurity and economic instability in Austin is not racially neutral:

| Group | % of County Pop. | Food Insecurity Rate | % in Food Desert Zip | Childcare Burden (% income) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| White Non-Hispanic | 48.2% | 10.2% | 8.4% | 14.1% |
| **Hispanic / Latino** | **26.4%** | **21.8%** | **42.6%** | **31.4%** |
| **Black / African American** | **8.1%** | **19.6%** | **51.3%** | **34.2%** |
| Asian / Pacific Islander | 7.3% | 9.8% | 12.4% | 16.8% |
| Multiracial | 4.8% | 14.2% | 22.7% | 22.1% |

Black residents are **1.9x more likely** to live in a food desert zip code than the county average. Hispanic/Latino residents have a food insecurity rate **more than double** that of White Non-Hispanic residents.

### 5.2 Intersectionality: Where All Three Crises Converge

The most acute vulnerability sits at the intersection of all three challenges: households that are:
1. Living in a food desert zip code (poor grocery access)
2. Earning below living wage
3. Bearing childcare costs without subsidy access

Estimated households in Travis County meeting all three criteria: **~18,000–22,000 households**, disproportionately Black and Latino, concentrated in the eastern crescent of the city (78721, 78724, 78741, 78742, 78744, 78753).

### 5.3 Historical Context: Policy Roots of Geographic Inequality

Austin's current geographic distribution of poverty and food access gaps is not accidental. The 1928 Austin City Master Plan explicitly relocated Black residents east of Chicon Street, concentrating city services (schools, parks) in that area to enforce segregation. This legacy is still visible in the spatial distribution of:
- Grocery store locations (historically HEB and major chains avoided deep East Austin)
- Transit routes (designed to serve downtown-to-north corridors, not east-to-grocery)
- Park and recreation amenities
- School quality and stability

Any serious food access equity strategy must acknowledge and name these historical roots.

## 6. Policy Recommendations

### 6.1 Food Access

**Immediate (0–12 months)**
- Expand mobile food pantry routes and frequency in 78724, 78725, 78742, and 78721 using City fleet resources or nonprofit partnerships (Central Texas Food Bank)
- Increase SNAP outreach funding in eastern zip codes; partner with WIC offices, schools, and community health workers for co-enrollment
- Adopt a formal City policy against new dollar-store-only food retail in food desert census tracts (several cities including Pittsburgh have enacted similar restrictions)

**Medium-Term (1–3 years)**
- Leverage City economic development incentives (property tax abatements, infrastructure investment) to attract full-service grocery to Colony Park (78724) and Montopolis (78742)
- Expand Cap Metro Route redesign to improve grocery connectivity from eastern residential clusters to existing HEB/Walmart anchors
- Fund community/co-op grocery models in deep food deserts where commercial retail is unlikely (similar to the Northgate Co-op model in other cities)

### 6.2 Economic Stability / Wages

**Immediate**
- Advocate for Travis County and City of Austin to adopt a local living wage ordinance covering all City contractors (building on existing $22/hr for direct employees)
- Expand Austin's Emergency Rental and Utility Assistance programs; current funding is insufficient for the scale of cost-burden households

**Medium-Term**
- Work with the Austin Chamber and major employers to advance voluntary living wage commitments tied to economic development incentives
- Support state-level legislative advocacy for Texas minimum wage increase (currently stalled; Austin delegation should coordinate with Harris County and Dallas partners)

### 6.3 Childcare

**Immediate**
- Increase City of Austin investment in the Child Care Relief Fund to expand subsidy slots beyond the current waitlist backlog; target 2,000 new subsidized slots in Year 1
- Require childcare needs assessments as part of all major commercial and mixed-use development permits (>200 units or >50,000 sq ft)

**Medium-Term**
- Develop a City-owned or City-anchored early learning center in Colony Park or a comparable eastern neighborhood as a proof-of-concept for municipal childcare infrastructure
- Advocate for federal expansion of the Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit refundability, and for Texas to adopt a state-level supplement
- Incentivize employer-sponsored childcare through City economic development agreements; model: employer provides on-site or near-site care in exchange for enhanced permitting or tax treatment
